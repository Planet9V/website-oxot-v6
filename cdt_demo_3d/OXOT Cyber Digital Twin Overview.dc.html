Why a model rather than a report

A report states a conclusion. A model can be interrogated, and it can be wrong in ways you can see.

When we say cell isolation is worth $44M of a $50M exposure, that number comes from walking the actual dependency graph — and you can open the model and watch the propagation stop at the cell boundary. When we say no safety instrumented system is reachable from any entry point, that is a query across 1,155 connections, not an assurance.

It also means the assumptions are visible. Every figure is marked as sourced, derived, or assumed — and each assumption is listed with the consequence of it being wrong. A reader who disagrees can see exactly which numbers move.

One dataset

The asset register, the ZCR diagram, the threat pathways, the consequence figures and the executive dashboard are views of the same data. A correction on the floor propagates to every artifact at once.

Two directions

Top-down consequence figures for executives and risk ledgers. Bottom-up engineering detail for controls, operations and network teams. Same model, so the two never contradict each other.

Defensible

A formal trail for IEC 62443 SL-C determinations — including where SL-A was negotiated down, on what rationale, and with which residual risk accepted by whom.

It outlives us

You keep the model. Add a system, patch a firmware, change a firewall rule — re-run the queries and the answers move with the facility instead of ageing on a shelf.

The workshops

Where the model meets the facility

Both workshops are run against the live model on screen, not slides. Disagreement is the point — every correction goes straight into the dataset and every downstream artifact moves with it.

1 Workshop one
Initial Risk Assessment
Validate the model, frame the threat, agree the target
On the agenda

We walk the model with your team, then walk the floor. What we drew from the documents and what is actually installed are rarely the same thing — the model is corrected to as-is in the room.

Then the threat landscape. TS 50701 works from worst case; we instead use comparative threat — our Attacker Threat Quotient, scoped to your sector, equipment class, facility type and geography. That gives a defensible threat basis rather than a hypothetical one.

We close by organising assets into a registry, marking those carrying SIL ratings, and setting initial SL-T by zone.

2 Workshop two
Risk Alignment
Show the consequence, agree what is reasonable
On the agenda

We run TACAM against the model — threat actors and pathways, carrying the typical vulnerabilities of each equipment class — and show where those paths land: fat-tail losses, safety functions, critical process.

Then the controls the SL-T ratings demand, and why each matters — argued from the pathway on screen and the consequence at the end of it, not from a checklist.

Architecture gaps get tested against ALARP / SFAIRP to arrive at a reasonable SL-C. The output is the ZCR diagram — the flat network view of the same 3D model, with zones and conduits agreed.

The reference model

A hyperscale AI datacentre, in full

We built one to show the method on something hard: a two-cell campus with liquid-cooled GPU racks, a full power train from the grid intake down to the rack tray, and every control network that ties them together.

Each cell is independently deployable — its own MV/LV distribution, CDU cluster, BMS zone controller, fire detection and suppression, and OT network segment. That claim is tested rather than asserted: a compromise of Cell A's OT segment reaches four assets and stops at two boundaries; a Cell A busway loss stops 67 assets and no Cell B rack.

Zoom to a single tray, trace a Modbus path from a workstation to a chiller, or fail a CDU and watch the wave propagate. It is the same model we build for a client — only the name on it is ours.

Power train

Grid intake, MV switchgear, transformers, four UPS models in 2N, battery room, generators with bulk fuel, BESS, per-cell busway to rack PDU.

Cooling

Central chillers, cooling towers, dry-cooler economiser, row CDUs with direct-to-chip loops, CRAH wall, and the condenser loop between them.

IT & network

Carrier demarcs north and south, DDoS and edge, firewall pair, dual spine, per-row distribution to ToR, jumpbox, out-of-band console.

OT boundary

Per-cell OT switch, OT firewall, data diode and protocol gateway, with DCIM and passive IDS monitoring from the NOC.

Life safety

Per-cell fire panel and VESDA aspirating detection, suppression bottle banks, EPO, and the hardwired interlocks that bypass the network entirely.

Physical security

Perimeter detection, mantrap and biometrics, cameras to a storage server, door contacts, badge issuance at the security officer's desk.

188 of the 1,155 connections are hardwired or serial — dry contacts, 4–20 mA loops, RS-485, Modbus RTU. They carry no network path, which is exactly why they are usually missing from an assessment and exactly why they matter when one fails.
