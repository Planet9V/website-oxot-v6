THE PROBLEM
01 / 07

Security tools that don't know what a valve does.

OT and industrial security inherited its playbook from IT — firewalls, patch cadence, network monitoring. None of it was built to understand that patching this system might disable a safety interlock, or that this “low-severity” vulnerability sits on the one asset that would shut down your whole facility if it failed.

THE GAP
A vulnerability scanner can tell you a device is running old firmware. It can't tell you that device also happens to control cooling for your most critical row of equipment — or that a failure there cascades into three other systems before anyone notices.
WHERE REAL RISK HIDES
That gap — between what a security tool sees and what actually happens on your facility floor — is where real risk hides. Not because anyone missed something. Because the tool was never built to see it in the first place.
WHAT THIS COSTS TEAMS, CONCRETELY
Without a model of physical consequence, every security decision becomes a negotiation nobody can win cleanly: patch now and risk the maintenance window, or wait and carry the exposure. Isolate the system, or keep the safety logic intact. These decisions get made — but usually on judgment and urgency, not on an actual number for what's really at stake.

HOW IT THINKS
03 / 07

A real number, not a checklist score.

Most security and compliance tools give you a score against a generic standard — a percentage, a letter grade, a checklist completion rate. OXOT gives you something a budget conversation can actually use: a defensible number, built from your real equipment and its real failure history — not an industry average.

HOW IT'S BUILT
Underneath, the twin runs the same kind of engineering analysis reliability and safety engineers already trust — failure mode analysis, reliability figures, hazard assessment — and combines it with live threat intelligence: which vulnerabilities are actively exploited, which ones matter for your specific equipment, and which ones are just noise. The result isn't a guess dressed up as a score. It's a calculation: how likely is this, and what would it actually cost.
WHY GENERIC BEST PRACTICE ISN'T ENOUGH
An industry checklist tells you what most facilities like yours should probably do. It can't tell you what your facility should do — because it doesn't know your equipment, your layout, your history. Two facilities can look identical on a compliance checklist and have completely different actual risk. The twin is built from your facility, not a template of one.
For the engineers who want the specifics: FMECA and reliability analysis, IEC 62443 zone and target security-level modelling, and CVE/exploit-intelligence matching, feeding an actuarial-style loss calculation. We're happy to walk through the full methodology in a briefing.

THE OUTPUT
05 / 07

NOW · NEXT · NEVER

Instead of a queue of alerts ranked by generic severity, you get a ranked list of what to fix, priced in the terms that matter to whoever's asking — downtime hours for operations, euros for finance, safety margin for engineering.

NOW
Reachable, exploited, breaks a minimum requirement.
Known-exploited vulnerability on an asset with a live pathway to a safety or cooling function. Highest loss avoided per euro. Fix inside the change window.
NEXT
Real, but bounded — or the path is long.
Meaningful ALE reduction that does not justify an emergency outage. Scheduled into the next maintenance window, with a compensating control until then.
NEVER
Unreachable, or the fix costs more than the loss.
Accepted with reasoning recorded against the object in the twin. The most valuable column — it is what lets your team defensibly stop working on the other 400 findings.
A WALKTHROUGH · ONE EXPOSED DEVICE, ONE REAL ANSWER

What the twin actually does.

SCENARIO
A data centre's cooling control system — a real IEC 62443 zone, with dozens of connected devices. One of those devices shows up with a known, actively-exploited vulnerability.
TODAY
A typical monitoring tool flags it: “vulnerability detected, high severity.” That's it. Someone has to figure out, manually, what that device actually does, what it's connected to, and whether it matters.
ON THE TWIN
The same exposure shows something different: this device sits inside the cooling zone for a specific row of equipment. Simulating a compromise traces the path it could take — what it can reach, what safety margin exists before there's a real operational consequence, and what it would cost if that path were used. That consequence, not just the severity rating, is what ranks it against everything else.
RESULT
The fix might be the same either way — patch the device. But now it's not one alert among hundreds. It's a specific, ranked, justified decision: fix this one first, and here's why.
