1 · Hero and positioning

Hero — headline + subhead
Your facility, as a model that answers questions

A Cyber Digital Twin is a working model of your facility — every asset, every connection, every protocol — loaded with your own safety and reliability data. Ask it what a compromised workstation can reach, what a cooling failure costs per hour, and which control actually breaks the path. It answers from your architecture, not from a benchmark.

Alternate hero — for a more technical audience
IEC 62443 assessment, grounded in your architecture

We build a queryable model of your facility from your HLD, DLD and interface control documents, correct it against the facility on a floor walk, and load it with your FMEA, hazard log and minimum operating requirements. Threat pathways, consequence, and SL-C rationale then come out of one dataset — traceable, defensible, and the same for every team.

Positioning — three-sentence version

Most OT security assessments produce a document. We produce a model. Because the asset register, the network diagram, the threat pathways, the consequence figures and the executive dashboard are all views of one dataset, a correction on the floor propagates to every artifact at once — instead of being reconciled by hand across documents that drift apart the moment the engagement ends.

Why a model rather than a report

A report states a conclusion. A model can be interrogated, and it can be wrong in ways you can see. When we say cell isolation is worth $44M of a $50M exposure, that number comes from walking the actual dependency graph, and you can open the model and watch the propagation stop at the cell boundary. When we say no safety instrumented system is reachable from any entry point, that is a query result across 1,155 connections, not an assurance.

It also means the assumptions are visible. Every figure is marked as sourced from your documents, derived from the model, or assumed — and the assumptions are listed with the consequence of each being wrong. A reader who disagrees can see exactly which numbers move.

2 · The model

Section intro — the reference model
A hyperscale AI campus, modelled end to end

Our reference twin is a hyperscale AI datacenter: one data hall of 96 liquid-cooled GPU racks at 120 kW each, organised into two independently operable cells. It carries 449 assets and 1,155 connections — from the utility feed and MV switchgear through to the cold-plate manifold in a single rack, and from the carrier demarcation through to a dry contact on a fire panel.

Every connection knows what flows over it and how it is carried. That distinction is what makes the model useful: of those 1,155 connections, 525 are physical flow — pipe, duct, busway, cable — and a further 188 are hardwired or serial. None of them can be reached remotely, and establishing that boundary early is what stops a threat assessment from treating the whole facility as uniformly exposed.

Cell architecture — copy for the differentiator section

Hyperscalers divide campuses into self-contained cells: 5–10 MW blocks that can be deployed, commissioned and operated independently. Each cell in the model has its own MV/LV distribution path, its own CDU cluster, its own BMS zone controller, its own fire detection and suppression, and its own OT network segment. Generation is shared; distribution is not.

Modelling that properly is what makes the security argument measurable. A foothold inside one cell's OT segment reaches two assets, because the cell VLAN has no inter-cell routing and terminates at a one-way diode. The same compromise on a shared management plane reaches the whole campus. A busway loss bounded to one cell costs $720K per hour; unbounded, it costs $10.44M. The isolation is not an assertion in a slide — it is the difference between two query results.

Explorer intro
Explore the twin the way we build it

The same model, four ways. Start with what exists, then add threat, then add zones and requirements, then add assurance. Each view carries only the controls that phase uses, so nothing is shown before the work that justifies it.

1 · Building

What exists, and how it connects. The model as built from your high-level design, detailed design and interface control documents. Search any asset, filter by service to see what flows where, and open the asset list by system to walk the register. Protocol filtering separates the routable from the hardwired — the first thing that shapes everything after it.

Controls: viewpoint · view · search · service · asset list · protocol

2 · Workshop — TVA

Threat and vulnerability, on the model. Pick an entry point and watch the pathway walk forward over routable protocols only — hardwired, analogue, serial and one-way links stop it, and the boundary that stopped it is named. Then fail an asset and watch the loss propagate through the facility's real dependencies, with the consequence costed per hour. Zones and conduits get their first cut here.

Adds: threat paths · cascading failures · IEC 62443 zones · conduits

3 · ZCR

Zone and conduit requirements. Colour the model by zone and see the boundaries as they actually fall. Every critical conduit is filterable, with its protocol, direction and the zone crossing it makes. The risk portfolio carries annualised loss by node and the controls that reduce it, with cumulative return. Assumptions and provenance are on the same screen — what is sourced, what is derived, what is still unconfirmed.

Adds: marker colour · risk portfolio · assumptions and provenance

4 · Assurance

Everything, in one view. The full control set for gap analysis against SL-A, the target reference architecture, and the reporting pack. This is the view a facility team keeps after the engagement — the model stays queryable, so the next change to the facility is a change to the twin rather than the start of another assessment.

5 · The method

Process section intro
Three phases, two workshops, three floor walks

We build from your documentation, correct the model against the facility, then load it with your safety and reliability data before recommending a single control. The order matters: a threat pathway is only worth discussing once it can be traced to a physical consequence your own hazard log recognises.

Phase copy

Phase 1 — gather artifacts and build the model

We build from your own documentation: high-level design, detailed design, interface control documents and equipment schedules. From those we derive the asset list, establish every connection, and classify the services and protocols each one carries. Where a document states something, we do not infer it.

Workshop 1 — review, walk the floor, frame the threat

We review the model with your teams and walk the floor to confirm it against the as-is facility, adjusting wherever documentation and installation disagree. Then we frame the threat landscape. CLC/TS 50701 works from worst case; we instead apply comparative sector threats through our Attacker Threat Quotient, weighted by client profile, equipment and facility type, and geography. The output is the initial SL-T zone and conduit cut, and an asset registry with SIL-rated items marked.

Phase 2 — load safety, reliability and operations data

We take your safety team's FMEA, hazard log and safety critical item list; your reliability team's critical item list; and your ConOps with its minimum operating requirements. Failure modes, impacts and consequences go into the model, so that a pathway can be traced through to an outcome your own teams already recognise.

Workshop 2 — threat actors, pathways and reasonable controls

We show threat actors and pathways on the model through TACAM, which carries typical vulnerabilities by equipment class, and trace them to fat-tail losses, critical losses and safety consequences using our own ALE model. We review the controls your SL-T ratings require and why they matter — grounded in the pathways and consequences on screen, not a generic catalogue. Architectural gaps are then tested against ALARP/SFAIRP to arrive at a reasonable SL-C, and the result is drawn as the ZCR diagram: the flat network view of the same 3D model.

Phase 3 — raise fidelity, verify, report

We enter SL-C and the security requirements for every asset by zone and produce the gap analysis. We then ask for samples and configurations, SBOMs and HBOMs, and switch and firewall configurations to raise the model's fidelity to the real facility — and walk the floor once more with the improved model to confirm it still matches. You receive a NOW/NEXT/NEVER report, a target reference architecture to IEC 62443, the SL-C to SL-A gap analysis, and a prioritised roadmap with quick wins.

The point of a common dataset is prioritisation. The consequence-driven top-down view builds awareness tied to numbers; the bottom-up engineering, controls and operations view earns trust with the people who run the facility. You need both to agree on where resources go — and both need to be defensible when an SL-C determination is negotiated down to SL-A.
