**Four decisions this makes answerable** (p.1, framed as a 4-column table):

3. **Can we change this safely?** — "Test the firewall, the re-zoning or the patch in the replica before the plant."
4. **What can we ignore?** (highlighted/orange, appears to be the standout differentiator) — "A written, defensible decision to leave alone what cannot reach anything that matters."

**"The problem it answers"** (p.1): *"Industrial plants were not built for today's cyber reality."*
> "Ageing equipment, asset records that disagree with each other and ever more connection between the plant and the office make it hard to see where the real risk sits. And unlike office IT, the fix carries risk of its own: improvements have to be made in a live plant where stopping is not an option."

Pull-quote: *"The hard part is not finding security issues. It is knowing which ones matter, which can safely be changed, and what to do first."*

Three-step framework (p.1 footer, repeated across doc as structural spine):

## Every Claim About Capabilities / Differentiators

**Decision 01 — Prioritization (p.2, "Three answers, and the discipline to use the third")**
> "Every operator has a backlog too long to finish and no defensible way to order it. We sort findings by what would physically happen if they were used against you, weighed against whether anyone could actually reach them."
> "How we know: the consequence comes from your own safety and reliability studies, and reachability from a model of your real network. Neither is our opinion."

Three-tier triage system — **NOW / NEXT / NEVER**:
- **NOW** ("Could end you"): "Something an attacker can reach that protects life or keeps the plant running. Not a budget conversation."
- **NEXT** ("Worth the money"): "Real exposure you could survive, ordered by how much risk each euro removes."
- **NEVER** ("Written off, on record"): "Unreachable, or reachable with no consequence. Documented with the reasoning, so it survives an auditor and a change of staff."

Pull-quote: *"NEVER is the one that changes how a team works. A backlog of four hundred items stops behaving like an unpriced liability the moment you can say, in writing, which of them cannot hurt you."* — Note: "four hundred items" is illustrative framing language, not a customer statistic; flag for fact-check/do-not-cite-as-data.

**What you are left holding** (outputs, p.2):
- "One number for the board" — "The Consequence Index — a single organisational rating, priced in euros, trended over 90 days, that drills all the way back down to the component that caused it."
- "A cost, not a score" — "Expected annual loss in euros, with the rare severe outcome priced separately from the ordinary year — grounded in published breach and claims data."
- "Nothing you cannot take apart" — "Every figure traces to the evidence that produced it, and every outside source is cited beside the value it fed. Never underwrite a number you cannot open."

**What changes, and for whom** (persona table, p.2):
- **The board**: "Stops being asked to trust a maturity score, and starts seeing one figure that moves."
- **Engineering**: "Gets a queue for Monday that survives a challenge from the plant manager."
- **Procurement**: "Buys against a number, and can compare two proposals on the same basis."
- **Audit and insurers** (highlighted): "See the reasoning, not just the conclusion — including what was deliberately not done, and why."

**Reported as a 90-day trend, and it moves for three reasons** (p.2):
1. "You changed something — work completed, and the risk it removed"
2. "The plant changed — new equipment, drift, an undocumented alteration"
3. "The world changed — a new exploit, a supplier, a shift in conditions"

**Decision 02 — Investment/spend (p.3, "What should we spend, and what does it buy us?")**
> "Security spend does not reduce risk in a straight line. The first investments compound; then the curve flattens, and past a point more money buys almost nothing. We map that curve for your estate and find the ridge — where each additional euro returns the most, and where it stops paying."
> "That is the difference between a quantified risk and an actionable one: a recommendation with a ceiling on it rather than an open-ended ask."

**"The rare severe year, priced on its own"**:
> "Industrial cyber loss is fat-tailed: one attacker reaching one safety-critical system produces a loss far beyond the average, and averaging never surfaces it. Monte Carlo simulation returns a full range of outcomes with confidence intervals, and the conditional value at risk of the tail is stated apart from the central estimate."

Pull-quote: *"Survive first. Optimise second. A programme that lowers average risk while leaving the ruinous case intact has spent money and bought nothing."*

**What the ridge gives you** (p.3):
- "A bounded number" — "A spend recommendation with a stated ceiling, and the point beyond which more money stops paying."
- "Comparable options" — "Competing controls, vendors and sequencing scored against the same set of outcomes, so the comparison is like for like."
- "A roadmap that orders itself" — "Every finding carries the risk it removes per euro, so the sequence falls out of the model rather than out of a workshop."

**Where the euro figure comes from** (p.3, methodology transparency — strong differentiator/trust claim):
> "Every term traces to a published source rather than an internal assumption, and the citation is stored beside the value — so an actuary, an underwriter or a CFO can pull any figure apart."
- "How often — incident frequency for your sector, from published breach investigations"
- "How much — loss size by sector and company size, from published insurance claims studies"
- "It really happened — disclosed corporate filings, as anchors on the scale"
- "Where you are — governance, conflict and economic conditions around each site"
- "Who is active — the groups currently targeting your sector"

**What we do not claim** (p.3 — explicit, important honesty/positioning box):

**Decision 03 — Testing/simulation (p.4, "Buy the control after you have watched it work")**

**"Consequence first, then reachability, then a price"** (p.5, methodology explainer):
> "Most of the industry works backwards: start from a vulnerability score, then guess at the impact. Consequence has to come first, because it is the only part that is genuinely knowable — the engineering already tells you what happens when a specific thing fails, and which safety system is supposed to catch it."
> "Probability is then a question about routes through your network. Price is arithmetic."

Four-step model: **Step 01 Consequence** ("What physically happens, and what stops running") → **Step 02 Pathway** ("Whether anyone can actually get there from outside") → **Step 03 Probability** ("How likely that is, stated with confidence intervals") → **Step 04 Financial impact** ("Consequence times probability, in euros")

**"The loss is engineered, not estimated"** (p.5):
> "How much a failure costs is not something a security tool should be guessing at. Your own engineers have already worked it out and signed it off. We use their figures rather than inventing our own."
- Safety studies (FMECA) — "how each component fails and what follows"
- Reliability-critical items — "lost production and lost product quality"
- Safety-critical items — "mapped to the systems meant to protect them"
- Minimum operating requirements — "what has to keep running, and for how long"
> "A cyber route inherits the cost the engineering already carries. Nothing is invented at the security layer."

**"A route, not a severity number"** (p.5):
> "Every asset is checked against what is known about the flaws it carries — and then scored by whether an attacker could reach it through your actual network. A critical flaw on an unreachable asset is not a critical risk."
- Known to be exploited — "flaws attackers are using right now (KEV)"
- Likelihood of exploitation — "how probable an attempt is (EPSS)"
- Technical severity — "how bad it is if used, in context (CVSS)"
- Attacker methods — "the techniques that fit that route (MITRE ATT&CK)"
> "Reachability is decided by the model of your network, not by a scanner's opinion."

**A worked example (illustrative, explicitly labeled "ILLUSTRATIVE")** (p.5):

- Probability: "With that route open and the flaw in active use, a successful reach sits high in the range."
- Decision: **NOW** — "and brokering the vendor route closes it for far less than replacing the controller."

# Key Takeaways

1. **The core positioning has evolved between v1 and v2 of the Product Sheet**: v1 sells "visibility" ("Your OT environment, made visible"); v2 explicitly sells "decisions" ("Before you change and spend, replicate your plant" / "OXOT powers better OT security decisions"). This matches the Sell Sheet's internal framing that "every OT vendor sells visibility — we sell something a buyer has never been offered." Website copy should follow v2's decision-first framing, not v1's visibility framing.

2. **"WorldMonitor" is a real, named product/module for geopolitical-external-risk monitoring** that appears explicitly in v1 of the Product Sheet and in the V2 Specification Sheet's technical detail, but is unbranded (described only generically) in the v2 Product Sheet. This is either an intentional simplification for the external audience or a naming gap — worth clarifying directly with the client whether "WorldMonitor" should be a named feature on the live site.

3. **"Red Squadron AI"** (adversary emulation) and **"Seldon SLT"** (loss/likelihood transformer) are named sub-components found only in the technical Specification Sheet, not in either Product Sheet — these could be strong "under the hood" credibility features for a technical/how-it-works page, currently invisible to the general buyer-facing copy.

4. **The Sell Sheet contains an explicit "Not This" list of prohibited claims** that is essential guardrail material for any future website copy: never say "actuarial," "rating-agency," or "certified"; never imply live attack detection/response; no invented accuracy figures or customer names; never promise a timeline before drawings are reviewed. This should directly inform legal/compliance review of any new marketing claims.

5. **No public case studies, named customers, or third-party validation exist in any of the 4 documents** beyond the Dutch government / European Cybersecurity Competence Centre co-investment under "CIF-NL 2025." All sample figures (including the recurring "95% pre-spend risk reduction" callout) are explicitly disclaimed as "illustrative, not customer data" — any use of these on the live site needs sourcing/verification or must retain the illustrative disclaimer.

6. **OXOT's origin story is M&A/acquisition due diligence**, not organic OT-security product development: the Seldon Engine was built to assess "whole industrial estates... in weeks, without walking every site" on an "acquisition due diligence" deal clock. This is a distinctive, quotable founding narrative that differentiates OXOT from typical OT-monitoring-vendor origin stories, and the Sell Sheet explicitly instructs reps to "lead with the origin story, not the platform" for M&A/corp-dev buyers.

7. **Compliance scope is broader in the technical Specification Sheet than in customer-facing materials**: NERC CIP, TSA directives, and CFATS (all US critical-infrastructure regulatory frameworks) appear only in the Specification Sheet, while customer-facing Product Sheets lead exclusively with EU frameworks (IEC 62443, NIS2, CRA, TS 50701, AI Act/Machine Act). This may signal untapped US-market messaging potential, or may simply be technical completeness not yet promoted externally — worth a strategic conversation with the client.

8. **The core scoring/triage system (NOW / NEXT / NEVER) is explicitly formulaic**: "triage by consequence × exploitability" per the Specification Sheet — this precise mathematical framing is not stated this plainly in either Product Sheet and could sharpen how the site explains prioritization.

9. **Deployment/hosting language is inconsistent between documents**: the Product Sheet names "a single-tenant instance in an AWS service of your choice"; the Specification Sheet instead says "Single-tenant island, EU sovereign cloud or on-premises." These are not necessarily contradictory (AWS could be one flavor of "EU sovereign cloud"), but the specific vendor name (AWS) appearing in the Product Sheet while being absent from the more technical Spec Sheet is worth confirming with the client before repeating on the site.

10. **OXOT explicitly positions itself as complementary to, not competitive with, OT monitoring vendors** (Dragos/Nozomi/Claroty-type tools): "their inventory feeds our model. Position as the layer above." This is a key partnership/ecosystem message that could inform channel and integration-partner messaging on the site, distinct from how it should talk about traditional digital twins (positioned as inferior/different-purpose) or big-four consultancies (positioned as producing stale reports vs. OXOT's living model).
