How to read this pack

This is a sample. Each artifact below is truncated to at most twenty rows to show structure, format and the reasoning behind it. A live engagement produces the complete set — the full asset register, every conduit, every hazard, and the long-form report aligned to IEC 62443 and CLC/TS 50701.

The model is the single source. The asset register, the network diagram, the threat pathways, the consequence figures and the executive dashboard are all views of one dataset, so a change in the field propagates to every artifact at once rather than being reconciled by hand across documents.

1The facility

The subject facility is a hyperscale AI campus built on a cell architecture: the hall is divided into self-contained blocks that can be deployed, commissioned and operated independently. Each cell carries 48 racks at 120 kW, giving 5.76 MW of IT load and 6.91 MW total at PUE 1.2. Generation is shared across the campus; distribution is not.

2How we build it — the process

Three phases, two client workshops, and a floor walk at each end. The model is built from documentation first, then corrected against the physical plant, then loaded with safety and reliability data before any control is recommended.

1

Phase 1 — gather artifacts and build the model

We build from the client's own documentation: high-level design, detailed design, and interface control documents. From those we derive an asset list, establish every connection, and classify the services and protocols each one carries. Nothing is inferred where a document states it.

Produces  Asset register · connection graph · service and protocol inventory · 3D model
W1

Workshop 1 — review, walk the floor, frame the threat

We review the model with the client and walk the floor to confirm it against the as-is plant, adjusting where the documentation and the installation disagree. We then frame the threat landscape. CLC/TS 50701 works from worst case; we instead apply comparative sector threats through our Attacker Threat Quotient, weighted by client profile, equipment and facility type, and geography.

Produces  As-is corrections · initial SL-T zones and conduits · asset registry with SIL-rated items marked
2

Phase 2 — load safety, reliability and operations data

We take the safety team's FMEA, hazard log and safety critical item list; the reliability team's critical item list; and the ConOps with its minimum operating requirements. Failure modes, impacts and consequences are built into the model so that a threat pathway can be traced through to a physical outcome.

Produces  Failure modes on assets · consequence model · minimum operating requirements · operating modes
W2

Workshop 2 — threat actors, pathways and reasonable controls

We show threat actors and pathways on the model through TACAM, which carries typical vulnerabilities by equipment class, and trace them to fat-tail losses, critical losses and safety consequences using our own ALE model. We review the controls the SL-T ratings require, and why they matter, grounded in the pathways and consequences shown rather than in a generic catalogue. Architectural gaps are then tested against ALARP/SFAIRP to arrive at a reasonable SL-C, and the result is drawn as the ZCR diagram — the flat network view of the same 3D model.

Produces  Threat pathways · consequence and ALE · SL-C rationale · ZCR zone and conduit diagram
3

Phase 3 — raise fidelity, verify, and report

We enter SL-C and the security requirements for every asset by zone, and produce the gap analysis. We then request samples and configurations, SBOMs and HBOMs, and switch and firewall configurations to raise the model's fidelity to the actual facility, and walk the floor once more with the improved model to confirm it still matches the as-is plant.

Produces  NOW/NEXT/NEVER report · target reference architecture (IEC 62443) · SL-C to SL-A gap analysis · roadmap, priority actions and quick wins

Where the outputs go

The engagement ends with a common set of information rather than a single report. Executive briefings carry the readout; the long-form report carries the full detail aligned to IEC 62443 and CLC/TS 50701. Facility managers receive a SecRAC; where the sponsor is organisational, risk management receives input to the risk ledger. Reliability and safety critical items go back to the safety team's hazard log.

That gives IT, OT, controls, operations, network and security teams, facility managers, safety and reliability teams, and executives one dataset to work from. The consequence-driven top-down view builds awareness tied to numbers; the bottom-up engineering, controls and operations view earns trust. Both are needed to prioritise resources, and both are defensible — which is what an IEC 62443 determination of SL-C requires when it is negotiated down to SL-A.

Why this artifact carries weight

Of 1,155 connections, 525 are physical flow — pipe, duct, busway and cable, where the flow itself is the interface — and a further 188 are hardwired or serial: dry contact, RS-232, RS-485, 4–20 mA, CAN and Modbus RTU. None of those can be reached or defeated remotely. Establishing that boundary early prevents a threat assessment from treating the plant as uniformly reachable, which is the most common way these exercises overstate exposure.

What the pathways show

A compromised security officer workstation reaches 120 assets — the widest blast radius of any single seat, because four systems share one host. A foothold inside a cell OT segment reaches 2, because the cell VLAN has no inter-cell routing and terminates at a one-way diode. No Z4 asset is reachable from any entry point. Safety instrumented systems terminate at supervised dry contacts and one-way interfaces, so no routable path exists.

7Artifact A5 — consequence and cascading failure

A threat pathway is only meaningful if it ends somewhere. Failures propagate through the model's hard service dependencies — electricity, water, condenser air and fuel — with redundancy taken from the design where it is stated and inferred from feed count where it is not.

Scenario 1 — Cell A busway loss

The Cell A busway spine is lost. Propagation reaches 66 assets over four waves, including all 48 racks in Cell A. No Cell B rack is affected. At $15K per rack-hour the cell exposure is $720K in the first hour, $5.76M at eight hours.

Scenario 2 — Row 1 CDU loss

A row CDU is lost. Because internal pump redundancy does not survive loss of the unit, the row's water dependency is all-of rather than any-of — an authored override, taken from the design rather than inferred. Propagation stops 18 assets including 16 racks, at $240K per hour. The thermal clock is short: throttle at 45 seconds, protective shutdown at 90.

Method note

10Artifact A8 — executive briefing

The readout answers four questions in order: what could happen, how likely it is, what it would cost, and what to do first.

What we found

  • Cell isolation is the highest-value control in the facility. A busway loss bounded to one cell costs $720K per hour. The same event across a shared distribution plane costs $10.44M per hour. The isolation is worth roughly $44M of the $50M exposure in the largest node scenario — a stronger argument than any individual control's return.
  • The widest single-seat exposure is administrative, not industrial. The security officer workstation reaches 120 assets because BMS, fire graphics, CCTV and access control share one host. Splitting that seat costs less than most network controls and removes more reachable surface.
  • Telemetry integrity is the controlling property. The highest-RPN hazards are all spoofing deviations, where the operator is shown a plausible but false value and acts on it. The answer is out-of-band measurement the attacker cannot write to, not more monitoring on the same path.
  • Safety held in every scenario tested. No Z4 asset is reachable from any modelled entry point, and no cascade reached one. That is a finding worth stating plainly, because it is the boundary the rest of the argument rests on.
