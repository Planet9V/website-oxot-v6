# IT/OT architecture convergence: joining two estates without inherited assumptions

A composite case, illustrating one of the seven engagements OXOT is regularly asked to run — not a single identifiable merger. The pattern is real and recurs across sectors; the two organisations are not disclosed.

```keyfacts
Scope :: two OT estates, joined after a merger or reorganisation
Basis :: IEC 62443-3-2 zones and conduits, derived once for the joined estate
Risk :: the larger organisation's IT standard applied to the plant by default
Output :: one target architecture, not two coexisting ones
Fit :: survives production and maintenance in both estates
```

## The situation

Two operating companies became one, and their networking and security teams inherited the standard instruction that follows every merger: align the estates. In IT that usually means one directory, one endpoint policy, one network standard — and the larger or more recently modernised parent's IT team began applying exactly that logic to the OT side, because nobody had told them a control system is not a fleet of laptops. A vendor-mandated protocol on one plant's line did not fit the new corporate network segmentation model. A safety system's isolation, which had never needed documenting because it had never been questioned, was suddenly a non-conformance against a policy written for office endpoints.

## What OXOT did

Zones and conduits derived to IEC 62443-3-2 for the joined estate as one exercise, not two separate audits stitched together after the fact — because a plant that inherits an OT/IT boundary designed for someone else's estate ends up with a boundary that fits neither. Each facility's actual operational needs were traced first: what a changeover requires, what an OEM's access path looks like under pressure, where a safety system's independence had to be demonstrable rather than assumed. Only then was the joined architecture reconciled against the acquiring organisation's IT standard, so the standard adapted to what the plants needed rather than the other way round.

## The output

One target architecture for the joined estate, with the interim states drawn explicitly — because two plants under one company still run on different timelines for a year or more, and an architecture that only describes the end state gives the people running the plant nothing to work from in the meantime. Where the acquiring organisation's IT policy was genuinely applicable to OT, it stayed. Where it was not, that was written down as a documented exception with a reason, not silently overridden and rediscovered at the next audit.

## The through-line

A merger moves the org chart faster than it moves the plant floor, and a security architecture built for the org chart inherits assumptions nobody actually tested against production. Two estates converge cleanly when the target architecture is derived from what both plants actually need to keep running — not from whichever parent's IT standard happened to arrive first.
