# M&A OT due diligence: a whole estate valued on a deal clock

A composite case, illustrating one of the seven engagements OXOT is regularly asked to run — not a single identifiable transaction. Sector and shape are real; the deal, the counterparties and the numbers are not disclosed.

```keyfacts
Scope :: a target facility or estate, pre or post deal
Typical :: 1–2 days on site per facility, 1–2 weeks of passive telemetry
Lead time :: coordination starts 21 days ahead
Basis :: business impact, not CVSS
Output :: red flags, a pre/post split, integration gaps
```

## The situation

An acquirer with a deal clock needed an OT risk picture across a target's estate before the price was fixed — several facilities, not one, and not enough time to walk every one of them the way a standalone assessment would. The target's own documentation was incomplete, as it usually is: as-built drawings that predated the last two retrofits, an asset list built from memory rather than a floor walk, and no consistent answer across sites to the same question.

## What OXOT did

The same discipline used on every facility engagement, aimed at a transaction and compressed to fit it: documentation review across the estate, external exposure assessment, one to two weeks of passive network telemetry per facility, and one to two days on site at the sites that mattered most to the deal — triaged by production value and by what the target's own data could not confirm. Coordination started twenty-one days before anyone arrived, because a due-diligence window is not a schedule that survives improvisation, and a deal team does not get a second attempt at the site visit.

## The output

A risk picture categorised by business impact rather than by CVSS — the register a deal team can actually use, and one number where the acquirer needed one number. Red flags that needed answering before signing, stated in the terms the deal team already used: production hours, safety exposure, and what a compromised site would cost to bring back. Remediation split explicitly into pre-acquisition and post-acquisition, so the acquirer knew what they were inheriting versus what they were choosing to fix. Wherever a target facility's security posture would not meet the acquirer's own standard once integrated, that gap was flagged as an integration cost, not discovered a year later during the first audit.

## The through-line

A whole estate does not get the treatment one site gets, and a deal team knows that going in — the question is whether the sampling was honest about what it could and could not confirm. Same engineering discipline as every other engagement OXOT runs, aimed at a different clock: what can actually go wrong here, and what does it cost if it does, answered on the schedule the deal actually has rather than the one a full assessment would prefer.
