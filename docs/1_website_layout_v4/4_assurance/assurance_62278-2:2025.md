## The safety challenge

### A cyber event becomes a safety concern when it changes a safety assumption.

Railways are designed to fail safely. A signaling, CBTC, ETCS, PTC, interlocking, traction-power, or control-center disruption may result in a safe state such as restricted movement, a train stop, a route lockout, or degraded operation.

That safe state can still have serious consequences:

- Trains cannot move through a junction, terminal, tunnel, depot, or route section.
- Headways fall and passenger platforms become crowded.
- Dispatchers lose operational visibility or ability to recover quickly.
- Freight corridors become congested and interchanges are missed.
- Traction power cannot be switched, isolated, or restored as intended.
- Field response, diagnosis, configuration recovery, or return to service is delayed.
- A cybersecurity weakness undermines an assumption beneath a safety barrier.

> **A vulnerability is not automatically a safety risk. It becomes safety-relevant when it can reach, alter, delay, bypass, or invalidate a function or assumption that contributes to safe railway operation.**

## What IEC 62278-2:2025 adds

### A structured systems approach to safety.

IEC 62278-2:2025 separates the safety method from the generic RAMS process. Part 1 addresses the lifecycle and management process; Part 2 focuses on safety-related methods, system definition, hazard/risk analysis, safety requirements, allocation, and safety demonstration. [webstore.iec](https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/79793)

## System definition

### Define the railway system, its boundaries, and its operating conditions.

Safety analysis begins with the **system under consideration**. A railway system is not simply an asset list. It includes operational functions, people, procedures, physical environments, interfaces, external systems, operating modes, assumptions, and constraints.

### Example railway scopes

| System under consideration | Typical safety and cyber context |
|---|---|
| Interlocking and route-control environment | Route setting, train detection, signal aspects, configuration control, field power, engineering access |
| CBTC corridor | Zone controllers, train-ground radio, onboard interfaces, OCC, headway, degraded mode, maintenance tools |
| ETCS / ERTMS segment | RBC, balises, GSM-R/FRMCS, onboard integration, route data, key/certificate dependencies |
| Freight PTC territory | Back-office systems, wayside interface units, radio, dispatch interfaces, locomotive equipment, maintenance pathways |
| Traction-power SCADA | Substations, RTUs, PLCs, protective devices, remote switching, isolation procedures, field safety |
| Station or tunnel environment | Ventilation, emergency systems, platform screen doors, public address, CCTV, power, passenger flow, incident response |
| Depot and rolling-stock maintenance environment | Software loading, diagnostic tools, maintenance laptops, test equipment, depot SCADA, fleet availability |
| Railway operations-control center | Dispatch, ATS, CAD, traffic management, telecoms, identities, time services, emergency and degraded operating procedures |

## Hazard and risk analysis

### Model the chain from initiating event to hazardous condition.

The safety method begins with identifying hazards, their causes, relevant barriers, and their possible consequences.

```text
Initiating event
        ↓
Hazardous condition
        ↓
Potential accident or harm
        ↓
Safety barriers and operating controls
        ↓
Residual risk and acceptance decision
```

The initiating event may be:

- Hardware failure.
- Software defect.
- Configuration error.
- Human error.
- Maintenance error.
- Communications or power failure.
- Supplier defect or unavailable support.
- Environmental condition.
- Cyber compromise or unauthorized change.

### Cybersecurity is an initiating event—not a separate risk universe.

```text

        ↓

        ↓
Affected railway function

dispatch visibility • maintenance configuration
        ↓
Hazardous condition or degraded state
Incorrect, unavailable, delayed, untrusted, or restricted function
        ↓
Safety / RAMS consequence
Safety barrier challenged • safe stop • capacity reduction • recovery delay
```

## Safety objectives

### Define what the railway must prevent, detect, control, or recover from.

Safety objectives describe the intended safe outcome of the system in its defined operating context. They should derive from hazards and risk analysis rather than from a generic checklist.

### Examples

| Railway environment | Example safety objective |
|---|---|
| Interlocking | Prevent conflicting routes or unsafe route-setting behavior |
| CBTC / ATP | Preserve train separation and ensure safe response when trusted movement data is unavailable |
| ETCS / PTC | Prevent unsafe movement authority while retaining defined degraded operating modes |
| Traction power | Prevent unsafe energization, switching, or isolation and support controlled restoration |
| Station / tunnel | Maintain safe emergency operation, ventilation, evacuation support, and emergency communications |
| Depot | Prevent unsafe maintenance, software loading, energization, or train release to service |
| Freight operations | Maintain controlled train movement, crossing safety, dispatch awareness, and safe recovery during PTC or communications degradation |

### Cybersecurity assumptions beneath objectives

The Twin should make visible the security assumptions that support a safety objective.

```text
Safety objective:
Prevent unauthorized alteration of route-setting behavior.

        ↓

Safety assumptions:
- Configuration is authenticated and integrity-protected.
- Engineering access is restricted to authorized personnel.
- Vendor sessions are approved, recorded, and time-limited.
- Production signaling zones are segregated.
- Change packages are validated before release.
- A trusted baseline is available for recovery.
```

This gives safety and cyber teams a shared question:

> **Can a cyber pathway invalidate a safety assumption or bypass a barrier on which the safety objective depends?**

## Safety requirements

### Turn safety objectives into specific, traceable requirements.

Safety requirements define what the system, subsystem, component, procedure, operator, or external barrier must provide in order to control the identified risk.

```text
Hazard
        ↓
Safety objective
        ↓
System-level safety requirement
        ↓
Allocated technical, procedural, and operational requirements
        ↓
Verification and validation evidence
```

### Example

```text
Hazard:
Unauthorized configuration change affects signaling behavior.

Safety objective:
Prevent configuration change from compromising safe train movement.

System-level safety requirement:
Only validated, authorized, traceable configuration changes may be applied
to the signaling environment.

Allocated requirements:
- Engineering workstation: named-user authentication and least privilege.
- Network: restricted conduit to signaling configuration environment.
- Vendor access: MFA, approval, time-limited and recorded sessions.
- Configuration package: integrity protection and version identification.
- Maintenance process: dual review and change authorization.
- Operations: approved validation before return to service.
- Recovery: trusted configuration baseline and tested restore process.
```

### OXOT requirement traceability

```text
Hazard
        ↓
Safety objective
        ↓
Safety requirement
        ↓
Allocated asset / system / procedure / role
        ↓
Cybersecurity assumption or control
        ↓
Verification / validation evidence
        ↓
Safety argument and acceptance status
```

This prevents a common assurance failure: a safety requirement is stated in one document, a cyber control is implemented somewhere else, and no traceable link demonstrates that the control supports the safety requirement.

## Requirement allocation

### Safety is delivered by systems, people, procedures, and external measures together.

IEC 62278-2:2025 uses a systems approach because risk reduction rarely belongs to one device alone. A safety requirement can be allocated across technical architecture, operating procedures, maintenance processes, human roles, training, supplier obligations, and independent barriers.

| Requirement type | Possible allocation |
|---|---|
| Functional safety behavior | Interlocking, CBTC controller, ETCS/RBC, PTC subsystem, PLC, relay, onboard system |
| Security boundary | Firewall, segmentation, jump host, identity, certificate, remote-access policy |
| Configuration integrity | Engineering workstation, source/configuration repository, signing process, approval workflow |
| Recovery capability | Backup, spare, diagnostic tool, tested procedure, trained maintainer, vendor escalation |
| Safe operation in degraded mode | Operating rule, dispatcher procedure, driver instruction, route restriction, field intervention |
| Detection and response | Alarm, event logger, SOC, OCC, operator dashboard, maintenance alert, incident procedure |
| Supplier control | Contractual support requirement, secure-update obligation, firmware lifecycle, spare availability, field-service controls |
| Independent assurance | Review activity, verification plan, validation test, independent safety assessment, acceptance authority |

### Key message

> A cybersecurity control is part of the safety architecture when it protects an assumption, interface, configuration, or barrier required for safe railway operation.

## Safety-integrity context

### Make cybersecurity assumptions visible beneath safety-related functions.

Safety integrity concerns whether a safety-related function performs adequately under stated conditions. A Cyber Digital Twin should not calculate or assign SIL automatically. That remains within the accountable railway safety process.

The Twin can, however, expose dependencies that may affect the safety-integrity argument:

```text
Safety-related function
        ↓
Required integrity / performance objective
        ↓
Allocated subsystem and implementation
        ↓
Technical and operational assumptions
        ↓
Cybersecurity dependencies
        ↓
Verification, validation, and acceptance evidence
```

### Example dependencies

| Safety function | Cybersecurity assumptions that may matter |
|---|---|
| Prevent conflicting routes | Configuration integrity, authorized engineering access, controlled remote maintenance, trusted update path |
| Preserve movement authority integrity | Authentication, secure communications, time integrity, certificate lifecycle, access control, software integrity |
| Safely isolate traction power | Restricted SCADA/RTU access, command integrity, logging, validated configuration, secure remote support |
| Maintain safe train separation | Reliable signaling/CBTC/ETCS/PTC interfaces, protected configuration, network segmentation, managed failure response |
| Support emergency ventilation or station response | Integrity and availability of controllers, operator interfaces, command paths, power, monitoring, and incident procedures |
| Release rolling stock safely after maintenance | Controlled diagnostics, authenticated software loading, configuration management, test evidence, maintenance approval |

The Twin’s job is not to declare the integrity level. Its job is to ensure the dependencies and cyber assumptions supporting the safety claim are visible, reviewable, and monitored for change.

## Verification and validation

### Show that the control is implemented correctly—and works in the real railway context.

A safety argument needs both verification and validation.

| Activity | Core question | OXOT support |
|---|---|---|
| **Verification** | Was the requirement implemented correctly? | Links requirements to architecture, configuration, test records, firewall rules, access design, component versions, and review artifacts |
| **Validation** | Does the integrated system achieve safe behavior in the intended operating environment? | Models operational scenarios, degraded modes, system dependencies, recovery paths, interface behavior, and change consequences |

### Cybersecurity change example

```text
Proposed change:
Add segmentation between vendor-access network and signaling engineering zone.

        ↓

Verification:
- Firewall policy permits only approved flows.
- MFA and approval controls are configured.
- Required protocol paths are documented.
- Configuration and logging requirements are met.

        ↓

Validation:
- Fault diagnosis remains possible.
- Signaling recovery process remains viable.
- Required maintenance activity can occur safely.
- Degraded-mode procedures remain valid.
- No unintended control-center, field, or train-control dependency breaks.
```

This is where the Cyber Digital Twin provides distinctive value: it can test the candidate control against the modeled railway before the operational system changes.

## Worked example

### Test secure vendor access against the safety argument.

**Illustrative scenario — no customer data.**

A rail operator allows a signaling vendor remote access to diagnose and support an interlocking and associated engineering environment. The connection is operationally valuable because vendor specialists may be needed during overnight possessions or urgent fault recovery.

However, the current pathway includes persistent access and broad reachability toward engineering tools.

### Safety and cybersecurity model

```text
Safety objective
Prevent unauthorized configuration changes from affecting safe route setting.

        ↓

Safety requirement
Signaling configuration changes must be authorized, validated,
traceable, and applied only through controlled processes.

        ↓

Cybersecurity concern
Persistent vendor pathway can reach an engineering workstation.

        ↓

Potential safety assumption affected
Only authorized and validated engineering actions can affect the
signaling configuration environment.

        ↓

Candidate control
Brokered, named-user, MFA-protected, approved, time-limited,
recorded access through a segmented maintenance gateway.

        ↓

Twin test
Does the control remove persistent reachability while preserving the
diagnostic, validation, and recovery flows required for safe operation?
```

### Result

The Cyber Digital Twin can show:

- Which vendor pathway reaches which engineering and signaling assets.
- Which access and communication flows are actually required for support.
- Which segmentation or access controls close unnecessary routes.
- What dependencies remain for fault recovery.
- Whether a new control affects the operating, maintenance, validation, or return-to-service process.
- Which safety requirement, assumption, evidence artifact, and accountable approval must be updated.

> The result is not simply “remote access was secured.” It is an evidence-backed statement that the change supports the safety objective while preserving the conditions required for safe recovery.

## Scope and boundaries

### OXOT supports safety evidence. It does not replace railway safety authority.

OXOT can support IEC 62278-2:2025-oriented work by modeling system boundaries, hazards, dependencies, cyber assumptions, safety-requirement allocation, candidate controls, evidence links, and change impacts.

However:

- OXOT does **not** create or approve a formal railway safety case by itself.
- OXOT does **not** assign Safety Integrity Levels or determine tolerable risk.
- OXOT does **not** act as an independent safety assessor, notified body, railway safety authority, regulator, or certification body.
- OXOT does **not** issue authorization to place a railway subsystem or system into service.
- OXOT does **not** guarantee compliance with IEC 62278-2, EN 50126-2, TS 50701, IEC 62443, or national railway requirements.
- Safety decisions, hazard-log ownership, acceptance, operational authority, and independent assessment remain the responsibility of the accountable railway organization and qualified parties.

> **OXOT provides the connected evidence and scenario model that supports safety, RAMS, cybersecurity, operations, and engineering teams.**
