## The best interactive centerpiece

Build one hero-level interaction: the **Decision Simulator**, not a visual gimmick.

### Interaction concept

The visitor sees a simplified industrial environment with three synchronized views:

- **Process / P&ID view:** equipment, safety functions, material/energy flow
- **OT network / Purdue view:** zones, conduits, devices, remote access paths
- **Decision / consequence view:** attack route, physical failure chain, loss exposure, and mitigations

A small control panel lets the visitor test a credible scenario:

```text
Scenario: Vendor remote access reaches a dosing-controller zone

Baseline
- Reachable route to a safety- or reliability-critical function
- Operational consequence appears on process layer
- Financial-loss range appears on decision layer

Proposed change
- Add segmentation boundary / broker vendor access / virtual firewall rule

Result
- Specific route neutralized
- Remaining routes identified
- Risk reduction and decision rationale shown
- Nothing changes in the real plant
```

This visually communicates the key distinction: you do not merely discover a CVE; you establish whether it is reachable, what it could cause in that particular facility, what intervention reduces it, and whether the benefit justifies the spend. 

Use synthetic, illustrative data. Never imply that a visually impressive demonstration is a customer-specific risk result.

## Product page framework

The product page should follow a narrative of **decision confidence**, not an enterprise-software feature catalogue.

### 1. Outcome-led opening

**Headline:**  
“Model the consequence before you approve the change.”

**Supporting message:**  
“Build a working replica from the engineering records you already hold. Simulate cyber pathways, operational cascades, and candidate controls without touching production.”

Immediately state the passive-first position: no agents on PLCs/RTUs/controllers and no active process-network scanning. 
### 2. Four buyer decisions

Give each decision its own substantial scroll section or subpage:

| Decision | Best visual | Buyer outcome |
|---|---|---|
| What do we fix first? | NOW / NEXT / NEVER triage board | Prioritization by actual consequence and reachability |
| What should we spend? | Risk-reduction curve and “ROI ridge” | A bounded investment recommendation rather than an open-ended security ask |
| Can we change safely? | Before/after attack-path simulation | Evaluate segmentation, firewall, patch, or vendor alternatives before production change |
| What can we ignore? | Defensible risk-acceptance record | Evidence-backed exceptions that survive audit and staff turnover |

The “what can we ignore?” message is unusually strong. Many vendors sell fear and endless remediation; showing defensible non-action distinguishes OXOT as decision-focused.

### 3. Explain why the answer holds

Use a scrollytelling chain:

```text
Engineering evidence
        ↓
Physical consequence
        ↓
Actual cyber pathway / reachability
        ↓
Threat and likelihood inputs
        ↓
Financial exposure and control options
        ↓
Traceable, drillable decision
```

Your source inputs are a major advantage: FMECA, hazard logs, SCIL/RCIL, minimum operating requirements, P&IDs, control logic, network topology, and OT protocols can ground the model in plant-specific evidence rather than generic severity scoring. 

### 4. Technical credibility layer

For CISOs, chief architects, engineers, risk teams, and M&A buyers, provide expandable technical modules:

- Facility physics and process constraints.
- OT assets, PLC/SCADA/RTU/HMI configuration mapping, and control logic.
- Purdue Level 0–4 modeling, segmentation verification, virtual firewalls, and PCAP/topology analysis.
- P&ID extraction and DEXPI 2.0 representation.
- Protocol support and integrations.
- CycloneDX-based BOMs: software, hardware, cryptographic, SaaS, and operational dependency views.
- Threat actor, vulnerability, and external-pressure intelligence.
- Monte Carlo loss modeling, annual loss expectancy, tail exposure, and traceable provenance.
- Compliance evidence outputs for IEC 62443, NIS2, CRA, TS 50701, and relevant sector obligations. 

Keep these as progressive disclosure—accordions, tabs, or a downloadable specification—so the primary product story stays buyer-readable.

## Visual design direction

Use **calm industrial intelligence**, not an aggressive “hacker” cyber aesthetic.

- Dark graphite or deep-blue default theme; a fully designed light mode.
- Cyan/teal for modeled or verified system state.
- Amber for decision attention and proposed scenarios.
- Red only for a constrained “critical consequence” state.
- Thin network and process lines, low-key technical grids, and map-like data visualizations.
- Real P&ID fragments, topology views, process equipment abstractions, and dependency graphs.
- Glass surfaces in moderation; high legibility must win over visual atmosphere.
- Motion should explain: route traversal, a simulated control closing a pathway, or before/after risk deltas.

The important motion is semantic. A line should animate because an attacker’s route is being traced or because a firewall simulation interrupts it—not simply because moving lines look cyber-themed.

Accessibility needs to be designed into this from the start: high text contrast, visible focus states, full keyboard equivalents for diagrams, and a reduced-motion mode. WCAG 2.2 calls for at least 4.5:1 contrast for normal text and protection from distracting persistent motion. [w3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html)
